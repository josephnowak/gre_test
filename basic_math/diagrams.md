# Diagrams

GRE data interpretation questions often use diagrams instead of plain lists of numbers. The first step is to identify what kind of display you are reading and what each axis, label, interval, or category represents.

---

## Column Charts

A **column chart** uses vertical bars to compare quantities across categories. The categories are usually on the horizontal axis, and the numerical scale is on the vertical axis.

Use a column chart when:

- The categories are separate groups, such as years, products, schools, or regions.
- You want to compare heights directly.
- The order of the categories matters or is natural, such as months from January to June.

**Example:**

Number of books read by four students:

| Student | Books |
|---------|-------|
| Ana | 6 |
| Ben | 9 |
| Carla | 4 |
| David | 7 |

In a column chart, Ben's column would be the tallest because he read the most books.

**Common GRE questions:**

- Which category has the greatest value?
- How much greater is one category than another?
- What is the total across all categories?
- What percent of the total comes from one category?

**GRE warning:** Read the vertical scale carefully. If each tick mark represents `5`, a column halfway between `20` and `25` represents about `22.5`, not `21`.

---

## Bar Charts

A **bar chart** uses horizontal bars to compare quantities across categories. It is mathematically similar to a column chart; the main difference is orientation.

Use a bar chart when:

- Category names are long.
- The chart compares several groups side by side.
- The focus is on ranking or comparing categories.

**Example:**

Sales by department:

| Department | Sales |
|------------|-------|
| Books | 120 |
| Electronics | 200 |
| Clothing | 150 |
| Furniture | 80 |

In a bar chart, Electronics would have the longest bar.

**Stacked bar charts:** Sometimes each bar is split into parts. For example, a sales bar might be divided into online sales and in-store sales.

| Department | Online | In Store | Total |
|------------|--------|----------|-------|
| Books | 45 | 75 | 120 |
| Electronics | 110 | 90 | 200 |

For a stacked bar chart:

- The full length of the bar gives the total.
- Each segment gives one part of the total.
- To compare one segment with the whole, use a fraction or percent.

**GRE warning:** Do not confuse the longest segment with the longest total bar. A category can have the largest total even if it does not have the largest individual segment.

## Tables

A **table** organizes data into rows and columns. Tables are usually the most precise data displays because they give exact values instead of visual estimates.

Use a table when:

- Exact numbers matter.
- You need to compare multiple variables at once.
- You need row totals, column totals, averages, or percentages.

**Example:**

| Store | Monday | Tuesday | Wednesday | Total |
|-------|--------|---------|-----------|-------|
| A | 12 | 18 | 15 | 45 |
| B | 20 | 16 | 14 | 50 |
| C | 10 | 22 | 18 | 50 |

Possible questions:

- Which store had the greatest total?
- What was the average Wednesday sales value?
- What fraction of Store A's total occurred on Tuesday?

For the Wednesday average:

```
(15 + 14 + 18) / 3 = 47 / 3 = 15.67
```

For the fraction of Store A's total that occurred on Tuesday:

```
18 / 45 = 2 / 5 = 40%
```

**GRE warning:** Check whether the question asks for a row, a column, or the whole table. Many mistakes come from using the right numbers from the wrong direction.

---

## Pie Charts

A **pie chart** shows how a whole is divided into parts. The entire circle represents `100%` of the total.

Use a pie chart when:

- The categories are parts of one whole.
- The question involves proportions, fractions, percentages, or angles.
- The total is known or can be inferred.

Important conversions:

```
Full circle = 360 degrees = 100%
1% of a circle = 3.6 degrees
Sector angle = percent of total * 360 degrees
Category amount = percent of total * total amount
```

**Example:**

A club has `200` members. A pie chart shows:

| Activity | Percent |
|----------|---------|
| Debate | 25% |
| Music | 35% |
| Sports | 20% |
| Art | 20% |

Number in Music:

```
35% of 200 = 0.35 * 200 = 70
```

Angle for Debate:

```
25% of 360 degrees = 0.25 * 360 = 90 degrees
```

**GRE warning:** Pie chart percentages should usually add to `100%`. If they do not, the chart may show rounded values, or one category may be missing.

## Line Graphs

A **line graph** shows how a quantity changes over a continuous or ordered variable, usually time. Points are plotted and connected by line segments.

Use a line graph when:

- The horizontal axis is time, distance, age, or another ordered scale.
- You need to identify increases, decreases, trends, or rates of change.
- You need to compare values at different points.

**Example:**

Temperature over five days:

| Day | Temperature |
|-----|-------------|
| Monday | 62 |
| Tuesday | 68 |
| Wednesday | 65 |
| Thursday | 72 |
| Friday | 70 |

From Monday to Thursday, the temperature increased from `62` to `72`:

```
Change = 72 - 62 = 10
```

From Tuesday to Wednesday, it decreased by:

```
68 - 65 = 3
```

**Slope and rate of change:**

On a line graph, slope represents rate of change:

```
Rate of change = change in y / change in x
```

If revenue increases from `$1000` to `$1600` over `3` months, then:

```
Rate = (1600 - 1000) / 3 = 600 / 3 = 200 dollars per month
```

**GRE warning:** A line graph connecting points does not always mean values between points were actually measured. If the chart gives monthly data, the line between March and April may simply help show the trend.

## Scatter Plots

A **scatter plot** shows the relationship between two numerical variables. Each point represents one observation.

Use a scatter plot when:

- You want to see whether two variables are related.
- You need to describe association, trend, clustering, or outliers.
- The data are paired values, such as height and weight or study time and score.

Types of association:

- **Positive association:** As `x` increases, `y` tends to increase.
- **Negative association:** As `x` increases, `y` tends to decrease.
- **No clear association:** The points do not show a consistent upward or downward pattern.

**Example:**

| Hours Studied | Score |
|---------------|-------|
| 1 | 520 |
| 2 | 550 |
| 3 | 570 |
| 4 | 610 |
| 5 | 640 |

This scatter plot would show a positive association: more study hours tend to go with higher scores.

**Line of best fit:**

A scatter plot may include a line of best fit, which summarizes the overall trend. The line does not need to pass through every point.

If the line of best fit is approximately:

```
score = 500 + 25h
```

then each additional hour `h` is associated with about `25` more score points.

**GRE warning:** Association does not prove causation. A scatter plot can show that two variables move together, but it does not automatically prove that one causes the other.

## Histograms

A **histogram** shows the frequency of numerical data grouped into intervals, also called bins. It looks like a bar chart, but the bars represent ranges of values, not separate categories.

Use a histogram when:

- The data are numerical.
- You want to see the shape of a distribution.
- You want to count how many values fall into each interval.

**Example:**

Test scores:

| Score Interval | Frequency |
|----------------|-----------|
| 50-59 | 2 |
| 60-69 | 5 |
| 70-79 | 9 |
| 80-89 | 6 |
| 90-99 | 3 |

Total number of scores:

```
2 + 5 + 9 + 6 + 3 = 25
```

Percent scoring from `70` to `79`:

```
9 / 25 = 36%
```

What histograms show:

- **Center:** Where the data tend to cluster.
- **Spread:** How wide the data range is.
- **Shape:** Symmetric, skewed left, skewed right, uniform, or clustered.
- **Gaps:** Intervals with no data.
- **Outliers:** Values far away from the main group.

**Histogram vs bar chart:**

| Feature | Histogram | Bar Chart |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Data type | Numerical intervals | Categories |
| Bar order | Ordered by number line | May be arbitrary |
| Bars touch? | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Main purpose | Show distribution shape | Compare categories |

**GRE warning:** The height of each bar is the frequency for the whole interval. If the interval is `70-79` and the bar height is `9`, that means `9` scores fall somewhere from `70` through `79`; it does not mean the score `70` occurred `9` times.

## Box Plots

A **box plot** shows how an ordered data set is split into four equal parts.

```
Minimum, Q1, Median, Q3, Maximum
```

These are called the **five-number summary**.

Text version:

```
min        Q1       median        Q3        max
 |---------|==========|===========|----------|
```

The box itself runs from `Q1` to `Q3`, so it contains the middle `50%` of the data.

Read it like this:

- `Q1` is the first quartile. About `25%` of the data are at or below it.
- The median splits the data in half.
- `Q3` is the third quartile. About `75%` of the data are at or below it.
- The box from `Q1` to `Q3` contains the middle `50%` of the data.
- Each quartile section contains about `25%` of the data.

**Important:** A longer section does not mean more data. It means that part of the data is more spread out.

### Two Useful Measures

| Measure | Formula | Meaning |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Range | `Maximum - Minimum` | Full spread of the data |
| IQR | `Q3 - Q1` | Spread of the middle `50%` |

Example:

For the five-number summary:

```
Minimum = 3
Q1 = 8
Median = 10
Q3 = 14
Maximum = 30
```

```
Range = 30 - 3 = 27
IQR = 14 - 8 = 6
```

The full data set spans `27` units, but the middle half spans only `6` units.

### How to Read Percentages from a Box Plot

In a standard box plot:

- About `25%` of the data lie from the minimum to `Q1`.
- About `25%` lie from `Q1` to the median.
- About `25%` lie from the median to `Q3`.
- About `25%` lie from `Q3` to the maximum.
- About `50%` lie inside the box, from `Q1` to `Q3`.
- About `50%` lie below the median, and about `50%` lie above the median.

**Important:** Equal percentages do not mean equal distances. A long section of the box plot means the data in that quarter are more spread out. A short section means the data in that quarter are more tightly packed.

**Example:**

```
Minimum = 0
Q1 = 10
Median = 12
Q3 = 14
Maximum = 40
```

From `0` to `10` contains about `25%` of the data.
From `10` to `12` also contains about `25%` of the data.
From `14` to `40` also contains about `25%` of the data.

Even though `14` to `40` is much longer than `10` to `12`, each interval represents about one quarter of the data. The long upper whisker shows that the highest quarter is much more spread out.

### GRE-Style Exercise

```
min        Q1                median     Q3       max
10         14                  x        62        75
 |---------|===================|=========|---------|
```

The box plot represents a data set containing `167` distinct values. How many values in the data set are between `14` and `75`, inclusive?

`14` is `Q1`, so it is the 25th percentile.

Find the position of `Q1`:

```
25% of 167 = 0.25 * 167 = 41.75
```

Since this is a position in an ordered list, round up:

```
Q1 is the 42nd value.
```

The values from the 42nd through the 167th are between `14` and `75`, inclusive:

```
167 - 42 + 1 = 126
```

Answer:

```
126
```

The value of `x` is not needed.

### Box Plot Traps

1. The box height has no meaning.
2. Longer sections show more spread, not more data.
3. A box plot usually does not give the mean or mode.
4. The median does not have to be centered inside the box.
5. A larger range does not always mean a larger IQR.
6. If outliers are shown as dots, the whiskers may not be the absolute minimum and maximum.

---

## GRE Tips and Common Traps

1. **Read the title, axes, units, and scale before calculating.**
   - Many wrong answers come from treating a scale interval as `1` when it actually represents `5`, `10`, or `100`.

2. **Know whether the display gives exact values or visual estimates.**
   - Tables usually give exact values. Charts and graphs may require approximation.

3. **Separate categories from numerical intervals.**
   - Bar and column charts compare categories; histograms group numerical data into intervals.

4. **For pie charts, identify the whole.**
   - A percent is only useful once you know what total it refers to.

5. **For line graphs, distinguish change from rate of change.**
   - Change is `new value - old value`; rate of change is `change in y / change in x`.

6. **For scatter plots, describe association carefully.**
   - Positive or negative association does not automatically prove causation.

7. **For box plots, separate spread from count.**
   - Longer sections show more spread, not more data. Each quartile section represents about `25%` of the data.

## Quick Summary Table

| Diagram Type | Best Used For | Key GRE Move |
|--------------|---------------|--------------|
| Column chart | Comparing categories with vertical bars | Read heights and vertical scale |
| Bar chart | Comparing categories with horizontal bars | Compare bar lengths and totals |
| Table | Exact row-and-column data | Track rows, columns, totals, and units |
| Pie chart | Parts of one whole | Convert among percent, amount, and angle |
| Line graph | Change over time or ordered input | Find increases, decreases, and rates |
| Scatter plot | Relationship between two variables | Identify association and outliers |
| Histogram | Frequency across numerical intervals | Read bins and distribution shape |
| Box plot | Median, quartiles, spread, and outliers | Use five-number summary and IQR |

| Formula / Rule | Meaning |
|----------------|---------|
| Pie chart sector | `percent of total * 360 degrees` |
| Line graph rate of change | `change in y / change in x` |
| Histogram frequency | number of values in an interval |
| Box plot five-number summary | minimum, `Q1`, median, `Q3`, maximum |
| Interquartile range | `IQR = Q3 - Q1` |
